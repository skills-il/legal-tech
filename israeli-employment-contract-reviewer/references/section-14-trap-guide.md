# Section 14 (Saif 14) Waiver Trap Guide

Section 14 of the Severance Pay Law 1963 (`סעיף 14 לחוק פיצויי פיטורין התשכ"ג-1963`) lets employers replace the statutory lump-sum severance with monthly pension contributions. Done right, it benefits both parties. Done wrong, it costs the employee tens of thousands of shekels.

## The Default Severance Rule

Without Section 14, Israeli law entitles a dismissed employee to one month's salary per year of service as severance (pitzuyei piturin), paid at termination.

Example: 5 years of service at NIS 20,000/month = NIS 100,000 severance at termination.

## What Section 14 Does

Since 2008 Section 14 applies automatically to the severance deposits the employer must make under the pension Expansion Order, so a contract does not need to invoke it. Before 2008 it applied only where a collective agreement or the Minister's approval provided for it. An employer depositing the full 8.33% may also notify the fund in writing, with a copy to the employee, that Section 14 covers the top-up as well.

Instead of accumulating a severance liability, the employer contributes 8.33% of salary (= 1/12) monthly into the employee's pension/provident fund. In exchange, the employee waives the right to statutory severance.

The employee keeps the accumulated 8.33% contributions regardless of how the employment ends (fired, resigned, mutual). The severance becomes portable like pension.

## When Section 14 Helps the Employee

- **Resignation**: under standard rules, resigners don't get severance. Under Section 14, they keep the accumulated monthly contributions.
- **Short tenure**: the contributions accumulate from the pension start date (day 1 for a candidate insured at hire, otherwise after 6 months) instead of depending on a qualifying dismissal.
- **Employer insolvency**: the money is in the employee's fund, not the employer's books. Safe from employer bankruptcy.
- **Portability**: the employee can move between jobs and keep the accumulated severance component.

## The Trade-off Most Guides Skip: Salary Raises

Section 14 also releases the employer from topping up severance for salary increases during the employment. Without it, severance on dismissal is computed on the LAST salary times years of service. With it, the employee gets what was deposited on each year's salary at the time. For an employee whose pay is expected to rise steeply, that is a real cost on dismissal, traded against portability and keeping the money on resignation. Say so before calling a full waiver a win.

## When Section 14 Hurts the Employee

**The trap**: if the "salary" definition for Section 14 purposes is narrower than the wage that statutory severance would be computed on.

Statutory severance is computed on the determining wage, which is built from base wage, seniority increment, cost-of-living increment, family increment and departmental or professional increment. A supplement that depends on a condition or a special factor is not part of it, and bonuses, overtime and expense reimbursements generally are not either. So the loss arises when the waiver leaves out a FIXED component that does enter that base.

Example: employee earns NIS 20,000 base + a NIS 5,000 fixed professional increment. If Section 14 only applies to the base (20,000), the monthly contribution is 20,000 * 8.33% = 1,666 ILS. The increment is not covered.

Over 5 years: the uncovered contributions come to 5 * 12 * (25,000 * 8.33% - 20,000 * 8.33%) = 5 * 12 * 416.50 = 24,990 ILS. Real money.

Do not run the same arithmetic on bonuses or overtime: because they generally do not enter the severance base at all, a waiver that excludes them costs the employee nothing against the statute. Asking for them to be included is a legitimate request for MORE than the law gives, and should be framed that way.

**The waiting-period gap.** Section 14 replaces severance only for salary on which contributions were actually made. Salary on which nothing was deposited, for example during a lawful 6-month pension waiting period, carries full statutory severance on a qualifying termination.

## The Proper Section 14 Clause

A Section 14 waiver should:

1. **Be explicit**: reference "Section 14 of the Severance Pay Law 1963" by name
2. **Be mutual**: include the employer's commitment to contribute 8.33% of the full "salary" as defined below
3. **Define "salary" to match the severance base at least**: base plus every fixed component that enters it (for example a seniority, professional or departmental increment). Adding expense-type allowances or recurring bonuses goes beyond the statute; ask for it as a benefit, not as a correction
4. **Reference the General Authorization** (`אישור כללי לפי סעיף 14`) issued by the Minister of Labor in 1998 (and updates), which lays out the exact conditions
5. **Specify the fund**: name the pension or provident fund the contributions go to
6. **State the start date** of the contributions, which follows the pension rule above

## Red Flags in Section 14 Clauses

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| Waiver applies to "base salary only" | Strips severance rights from any fixed component that enters the severance base |
| Waiver is one-sided (employee signs, employer does not commit to 8.33%) | Worst of both worlds - employee loses, employer keeps liability |
| Refers to 6% severance component when a FULL Section 14 waiver is intended | 6% severance is the legal mandatory minimum and is perfectly legal on its own. But a FULL Section 14 waiver (one that completely replaces statutory severance) requires the severance component to be 8.33%. At 6% the waiver only partially funds severance, so the employee can still claim the gap. This is not illegal, it just means the waiver is partial |
| Waiver applies retroactively to prior tenure | Cannot waive severance already accrued |
| No reference to the General Authorization | Not a defect by itself: Section 14 applies automatically to the mandatory deposits. Check the rate and the salary base instead |
| Employer deposits less than 8.33% to severance | Lawful. On a dismissal that carries severance, the employer must top up the difference between 8.33% and what was deposited, at the last salary |

## What to Negotiate

Push for:

1. **Full salary definition**: "Salary" for Section 14 purposes includes base + all fixed recurring components. Adding regularly paid bonuses is an above-statute ask, worth making but not a correction
2. **Full 8.33% severance rate for the waiver**: 6% is the legal minimum and is fine on its own, but only an 8.33% severance component makes the Section 14 waiver fully replace statutory severance
3. **From the statutory start date**: day 1 for a candidate who held pension insurance when starting, after 6 months otherwise, and never after a contractual trial period, which has no statutory basis and cannot delay pension
4. **Proper invocation**: explicit reference to Section 14 of the Severance Pay Law and the General Authorization
5. **Employer signature**: employer explicitly commits to the 8.33% contribution, not just "employee waives"

## Related: Section 45a Tax Credit on Employee Pension Contributions

Section 45a of the Income Tax Ordinance is a separate benefit from Section 14, but the two interact in payroll calculations. When a contract sets the employee's pension contribution at the mandatory 6%, the employee is also entitled to a 35% income-tax credit on that contribution under Section 45a, up to a maximum qualifying contribution of 8,148 NIS a year in 2026 (an average of 679 NIS a month, and in any case no more than 7% of salary), a figure unchanged since 2024.

Why this matters when reviewing a contract:
- If a contract quotes "take-home pay" or "net salary" numbers, verify the employer's calculation applied the Section 45a credit. Missing it overstates the employee's deductions.
- If the contract lets the employee contribute more than 7% of salary to pension voluntarily, flag that amounts above 7% do not receive the credit and become fully taxable.
- The credit is automatic on payroll for salaried employees, but only on contributions made via the payroll deduction (not on personal deposits from a bank account).
- The credit is distinct from Section 47 (deduction for pension contributions from tax-favored income). Most salaried employees use Section 45a; self-employed and personal contributions use Section 47.
